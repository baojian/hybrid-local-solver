Personalized PageRank (PPR) measures the relevance of graph vertices to a
seed through a geometrically stopped random walk. Its local computation
supports graph clustering and diffusion without requiring access to the
entire graph \citep{andersen2006local,andersen2007using}. Given a seed
vertex $v$, we seek a sparse approximation $\widehat\vpi$ to the lazy
PageRank vector $\vpi$ satisfying
$\norm{\mD^{-1}(\widehat\vpi-\vpi)}_\infty\leq\epsppr$, where
$\mD$ contains the original graph degrees. The central question is whether
acceleration can improve the total work needed to compute this local
approximation.

The classical push algorithm computes a sparse approximation in
$O(1/(\alpha\epsappr))$ degree-weighted work, where $\alpha$ is the
teleportation parameter and $\epsappr$ is its normalized residual
tolerance. Positive residual mass pays for each neighborhood scan. Global
accelerated methods suggest a square-root improvement: the symmetric
PageRank system has spectrum in $[\alpha,1]$, so accelerated gradient,
conjugate gradient, and Chebyshev methods need only
$\widetilde O(1/\sqrt\alpha)$ iterations. A full matrix-vector product,
however, can read the entire graph. A local running-time bound must also
control the vertices discovered and the cost of every repeated update.

\paragraph{Regularized PageRank and the acceleration question.}
Regularized personalized PageRank (RPPR) uses weighted $\ell_1$
regularization to give a sparse optimization formulation:
\begin{equation*}
 \min_{\vx\in\R^n}
 F_\rho(\vx):=
 \tfrac12\vx^\trans\mQ\vx-\alpha\eunit{v}^\trans\mD^{-1/2}\vx
           +\alpha\rho\norm{\mD^{1/2}\vx}_1,
 \qquad
 \mQ=\alpha\mI+\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\bm{\mathcal L}.
\end{equation*}
Here $\bm{\mathcal L}$ is the symmetric normalized graph Laplacian and
$\rho$ is the regularization parameter. The optimum is nonnegative and
has support volume at most $1/\rho$
\citep{fountoulakis2019variational}. Local proximal gradient exploits
this structure in $\widetilde O(1/(\alpha\rho))$ work.
\citet{fountoulakis2022open} asked for an accelerated local
algorithm with running time
\begin{equation*}
 \widetilde O\!\left(\frac1{\rho\sqrt\alpha}\right),
\end{equation*}
with only polylogarithmic dependence on objective accuracy and no dependence
on the ambient graph size. We attain this algorithmic target by two
different methods. An explicit accuracy conversion then gives the PPR
approximation bound.

\paragraph{The obstacle to local acceleration.}
Two costs have limited earlier approaches. Momentum can activate vertices
outside the optimal support, including vertices of very large degree;
standard FISTA can fail support confinement
\citep{fountoulakis2026complexity}. Methods that preserve safe support
expansion instead solve a sequence of restricted problems. The accelerated
sparse algorithm of \citet{martinezrubio2023accelerated} retains a factor
for the number of support discoveries. The recent active-set method of
\citet{wei2026simple} uses nearly-linear symmetric diagonally dominant (SDD) system solves
on each discovered set, but its total work still depends on the number of these sets.
Our deterministic method bounds the cumulative cost of an accelerated
trajectory. Our randomized method bounds how many restricted systems
need to be processed.

\paragraph{Our contributions.}
For a point source on a finite connected simple unweighted undirected
graph, we establish the following results.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Deterministic local acceleration.}
Regularization continuation and constrained accelerated corrections
attain additive RPPR objective accuracy $\epsobj$ in
$\widetilde O(1/(\rho\sqrt\alpha))$ work
(\cref{thm:deterministic-rppr}). The proof controls cumulative scanned
volume, including repeated visits and excursions outside the optimal
support. A sparse implementation realizes this bound, and a specified
bounded-arithmetic version gives explicit encoding costs.

\item \textbf{Randomized acceleration with support adaptation.}
Certified threshold batching attains the same objective guarantee with
expected work
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde O\!\left(V_*\min\{k_*,\alpha^{-1/2}\}\right)
 \leq\widetilde O\!\left(
      \min\{\rho^{-2},\rho^{-1}\alpha^{-1/2}\}\right),
 \label{eq:intro-random-adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $k_*$ and $V_*$ are the cardinality and original-degree volume
of the optimal support (\cref{thm:randomized-rppr-main}). This retains
the small-support regime of the active-set bound while adding an
accelerated bound on the number of batches. The algorithm is Las Vegas:
every accepted result is certified.

\item \textbf{A PPR approximation consequence.}
Regularization bias and objective error together yield a sparse
$\widehat\vpi$ with
$\norm{\mD^{-1}(\widehat\vpi-\vpi)}_\infty\leq\epsppr$ in
$\widetilde O(1/(\epsppr\sqrt\alpha))$ deterministic work, or the
same expected work for the randomized method
(\cref{cor:main-ppr}). The conversion specifies separate regularization
and objective-accuracy budgets. We also give a certified conversion from
the vertex sets discovered by these algorithms to PPR approximations,
using principal systems with the original graph degrees.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why the two methods remain local.}
The deterministic algorithm solves a sequence of decreasing regularization
levels. Each level starts from a safe sparse baseline and accelerates the
remaining correction inside a degree-scaled box with a mass constraint.
Two comparison energies for the same recurrence connect objective
convergence to local work. A signed-flow argument then charges the total
volume of the updated coordinates, rather than requiring every
intermediate support to stay inside the optimum's support. The sparse
implementation is part of the running-time analysis.

The randomized algorithm repeatedly solves on its current active set,
certifies the result, and admits boundary vertices whose violations exceed
a common threshold. The sign structure of the PageRank matrix certifies
that admitted vertices belong to the optimal support. The key question is how often this cycle repeats.
A block-Cholesky and Chebyshev argument gives
$\widetilde O(1/\sqrt\alpha)$ batches; the elementary cardinality
bound gives at most $k_*$. Combining the smaller bound with charged
SDD solves yields \cref{eq:intro-random-adaptive}.

Both methods count total local work under the access and arithmetic
model in \cref{sec:problem-formulation}. These results concern the stated algorithms; the
behavior of unmodified FISTA is a separate question. General seed inputs
and their additional costs are treated in \cref{app:seed-scope}.
